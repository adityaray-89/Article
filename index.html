The risk

A heritage at risk

India's heritage is vast and vulnerable. The stonework of Hampi and the murals of Ajanta have survived centuries, yet they now face pollution, climate stress, tourism pressure and neglect. Conservation can no longer depend on restoration alone; it must also include documentation, prediction and access.

Method · LiDAR

Measuring the invisible

Consider 3D LiDAR scanning. By firing millions of laser pulses and timing their return, it builds a millimetre-scale digital twin of a monument. Hairline cracks, tilting walls and gradual erosion become measurable, so conservators can act before damage becomes irreversible.

Step 1 of 3 Illustration  

Step 1

Fire the pulse

A scanner sweeps laser pulses across the monument, one sightline at a time.

Step 2

Time the return

Each reflection's travel time gives an exact distance to the stone it struck.

Step 3

Build the model

Millions of points join into a millimetre-scale 3D twin that keeps the monument's geometry even as the stone itself ages.

Case files

The idea has already proved its worth

At Notre-Dame, a laser scan made years before the 2019 fire became a precious reference for rebuilding. In Cambodia, airborne LiDAR exposed settlements near Angkor hidden beneath forest. And in India, scanning of Hampi's monuments began in 2015 so that the people protecting them would have exact data to work from.

France · 2015

Notre-Dame de Paris

Architectural historian Andrew Tallon scanned the cathedral inside and out with a laser in 2015. When the April 2019 fire damaged its spire and roof, the exact digital replica was described as crucial to any future rebuilding effort.

Source: MIT Technology Review
Cambodia · 2015

Angkor and the hidden cities

A 2015 airborne LiDAR survey, led by archaeologist Damian Evans of the Cambodian Archaeological Lidar Initiative, revealed hidden sites near Angkor, including a jungle city that may be larger than Phnom Penh.

Source: Smithsonian Magazine
India · 2015

Hampi, Karnataka

The Karnataka State Council for Science and Technology began 3D laser scanning of Hampi's monuments under the Union Science and Technology Department's India Digital Heritage Scheme. The two-year project aimed to give the Archaeological Survey of India the precise data it needed to protect the site; five monuments were already scanned when it was reported.

Source: Business Standard / PTI, April 2015

Method · Artificial intelligence

Imagination, with discipline

Artificial intelligence adds imagination, but it must be disciplined. By studying surviving fragments, archival photographs and related designs, AI can propose how damaged sculptures or missing architectural details may have looked.

Still, an algorithm's output is a hypothesis, not history, and it must always be labelled as one.

Method · Hyperspectral imaging

Chemical eyesight

Hyperspectral imaging works as a kind of chemical eyesight. By capturing light across many narrow wavelengths, it can identify pigments, recover faded inscriptions and separate original material from later repairs, all without touching a fragile manuscript or painting.

The limits

The limits of the machine

Yet technology has limits. Machines cannot interpret ritual, memory or meaning. That work belongs to historians, traditional artisans and local communities, who must guide every digital reconstruction. Four challenges deserve honest attention.

Cost

Scanning is expensive, and smaller sites risk being left behind.

Data stewardship

Digital files can be lost, locked away or misused, so ownership and long-term storage need clear rules.

The digital divide

A village without internet access may never see its own heritage in virtual form.

Authenticity

Reconstructions must separate evidence from guesswork, every time.

A living timeline

Centuries of heritage, new ways to preserve it

Monuments carry histories across generations. Digital tools add new ways to record, study and share them without replacing the original places or the people who care for them.

12th century

Angkor Wat

The Khmer temple complex becomes one of the great architectural landmarks of Southeast Asia.

c. 1199 onward

Qutb Minar

Construction begins in Delhi; the wider complex reflects layers of architectural history.

14th–16th centuries

Vijayanagara & Hampi

A major South Indian imperial capital flourishes, leaving a vast archaeological landscape.

1632–1653

Taj Mahal

The Mughal mausoleum is built in Agra, celebrated for its symmetry, stonework and inlay.

2010s

Digital documentation

Laser scanning and detailed digital records help document historic structures, including Notre-Dame before the 2019 fire.

Today

Heritage meets technology

3D surveys, photogrammetry, remote sensing and carefully reviewed AI tools can support research and conservation.
